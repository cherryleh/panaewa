Plant Profiles

Kalo

Colocasia esculenta

Other namesTaro, Talo, Dalo, Abalong, Imo, CocoyamKinolauKāne

Canoe plant, staple food, steamed and mashed, center of mythology/spiritualism/social structure.

Profile

Believed to originate in Asia. Corms are high in carbohydrates and contain calcium oxalate crystals that must be heated before eating. Leaves are rich in vitamin A/C and also contain calcium, iron, thiamine, and riboflavin. Propagated by cloning. Reaches maturity after 7-11 months. Corms average about 2-5lbs in weight. Different cultivars produce different qualities and characteristics. Production impacted by method of cultivation, weather conditions, water conditions, etc.

Vocabulary

  • Lau/ lūʻau (leaf)
  • Hā (petiole)
  • Huli (the part of the stalk used for propagating)
  • Kalo (corm)
  • ʻOhā (smaller corm/offshoot growing off of main corm)

Planting

Can be planted year-round in Hawaiʻi, but is best planted between April and September. Can be planted in multiple ways including loʻi, ridges/furrows, terraces, mounds, holes, etc. Kalo is typically planted in rows spaced roughly three feet apart. Plant in spaces with at least 10" of soil. Areas with good air circulation and day-long sunshine are preferred. New plots should be rested for at least one month before planting because decomposing green plant material will compete for Nitrogen with newly planted kalo (as will immature compost). Reused plots should be allowed to lie fallow for at least a few months, but up to a year if disease or pests are present. "Dryland" and "wetland" varieties should be used in appropriate settings. Fern fronds were traditionally used to protect dryland kalo beds during times of intense sunlight, and removed during periods of rain. Fertilize only for the first 6 months of growth. During the first 4 months of growth, weeds may outcompete taro for soil nutrients, so weed 2-3 times a month until taro leaves create a shade canopy. About 2" of course mulch at the base of the plant will also help suppress weed growth. Planting moons are Hilo, Hoaka, Kū Kahi, Kū Lua, Kū Kolu, Kū Pau, Mōhalu, Akua, Hoku, Māhealani, Kāne, Lono, and Mauli. Kalo plants used for their leaves were traditionally planted on Hilo and Hoaka moons. Kalo plants used for their corms were traditionally planted on Mōhalu and Hua moons.

Other Notes

In Hawaiʻi, "kalo" generally refers to Colocasia esculenta, but can also refer to the Xanthosoma, Alocasia, and Cyrtosperma genera. Taro root aphids are difficult to see due to their size but can be identified by the waxy white fluff that covers them. Infested huli can be treated before planting by soaking in a bath of 120-degree water for 6 minutes, then placing the huli in cool water. Apple snails will feed on the entire taro from leaf to corm and create openings for disease and other pests to enter the plant. Apple snails thrive in flooded fields and fresh water sources, so a dry-down period can help to clear an infested patch. Ducks are good for snail-control in flooded loʻi. Wear gloves when removing snail eggs by hand (bunches of small pink eggs on the petiole indicate apple snails). Vinegar can be used to relieve "taro itch" caused by calcium oxalate crystals if they come into contact with skin. A rinse of cold water, salt, and baking soda can also help with skin or mouth irritation. Use gloves when handling and cutting corms to prevent "taro itch." Names for individual cultivars varied from island to island, a single cultivar may have multiple names. Lūʻau is at peak production in summer and can be harvested every 2-3 weeks. During winter, harvesting can be done every 3-4 weeks. Removing leaves from the plant will slow development of the corm. Older plants produce tougher leaves that take longer to cook. Common diseases are leaf blight, soft rot (pala/ palahi), southern blight, leaf spot, pocket rot, dry rot, black rot, soft rot, dasheen mosaic, and loliloli.

Text Excerpts (19)

"Taro is indisputably the most important plant in Hawaiian culture. Besides being the staple food source, it was also the center of spiritualism, mythology, and social structure. In Hawaiian lore, the kalo plant is literally the elder brother of mankind. As the elder brother, kalo cares for and nourishes mankind, and we, in return, must respect and care for kalo. The kalo plant is high in carbohydrates, vitamin A, B, and C, and a variety of other elements and enzymes that are important to healthy living. Kalo contains calcium oxalate crystals in the leaves and tubers, and traditionally had to be steamed for over an hour before the crystals broke down and the plant became edible. Steamed kalo was then mashed into poi which was the staple food of old Hawaiʻi. Breakfast, lunch, and dinner usually centered on poi, and the signal to begin eating was the uncovering of the poi bowl. Kalo is typically cloned, with each progressive crop being derived from the last harvest." (Lincoln 2009 p. 84-85)

"Kalo and 'uala were the most important crops for the ancient Hawaiians. They furnished not only the carbohydrate base of their diet and goodly amounts of high-quality vitamins and minerals but also were kinolau of the gods Kāne and Lono, respectively." (Abbott 1992 p. 23)

"The myth of the origin of the Hawaiian people is known in several versions, but all of them begin similarly and give kalo a prominent place. When the sky principle Wākea coupled with the earth principle Papa, their first child, Hāloanaka, was stillborn, but a kalo plant sprouted from the place where the fetus was buried. Their second child was a daughter, Hoʻohōkūkalani, by whom Wākea later had a human son named Hāloa, the ancestor of the Hawaiian aliʻi and thus of all Hawaiians." (Abbott 1992 p. 23)

"Taro is full of needle-like and knob-like crystals of calcium oxalate that make it very painful to eat raw but which can be dissolved and rendered harmless by exposure to heat." (Abbott 1992 p. 25)

"Since kalo rarely produces seeds, it is always propagated in one of three other ways. The most common is to [use] huli from the upper six to eight centimeters (two or three inches) of the corm, containing the stem apex, and the lower ten to twelve centimeters (four to five inches) of the hā. The alternative methods are to plant buds borne in the axils of the leaves or to plant ʻohā, which are outgrowths produced by adventitious roots of the parent plant." (Abbott 1992 p. 26)

"In experiments with eight varieties of kalo, all maturing in six to twelve months, Dr. Handy found only one that needed to be harvested promptly at maturity. The other varieties could remain in the flooded loʻi for two to twenty-four months after reaching maturity. This feature of the plant makes kalo a very flexible crop and allowed loʻi to double as a storehouse. The planter could harvest what he needed, perhaps a little at a time." (Abbott 1992 p. 26)

"Dryland varieties take a little more time to mature than wetland kalo---eight to twelve months. Depending on the variety, they may remain in the soil three to twelve months beyond maturity, or even longer." (Abbott 1992 p. 27)

"All cultivars (cultivated varieties) of taro can be grown under non-flooded conditions, but not all can grow well in flooded soil. Ancient Hawaiians divided taro into wetland and upland types based on the place(s) and the cultivation system that best suited each cultivar." (Evans 2008 p. 29)

"Ancient Hawaiians were descriptive in naming the different cultivars of taro, and they often grouped them into families based on the plants' characteristics. Within large families, members were given a 'first' and 'last' name, similar to how a scientific botanical name is given; the first indicating the group, such as Mana, Lauloa, or Piko, that the cultivar belongs to (similar to a genus name) and the second describing a quality distinguishing the cultivar from the others, such as the fairest or the darkest, or the shortest or the tallest (in the same manner that a species name is given)." (Evans 2008 p. 34)

"[The] Lehua [family of taro] is known for its beautiful lilac-purple poi. Lehuas are also known as the 'royal taros' and were formerly grown for and eaten only by the aliʻi. This is the main type grown in Hanalei on Kauaʻi, with over 300 acres in production. High-yielding and early-maturing, Lehua taros are ready to harvest in 8-12 months, depending on the cultivar. A downside is they cannot be held in the patch once mature, because of their tendency to rot... [Lehua] are used for both flooded and non-flooded culture. Lehua ʻEleʻele is one of the fastest maturing of the Lehua family, at 7-8 months in some non-flooded situations." (Evans 2008 p.35)

"Mana means 'branching' and refers to branching corms, a characteristic peculiar to [the Mana] family [of taro]. These taros have a rubbery consistency when made into kūlolo, and they make poi of a fair quality and are excellent as table taro. This is one of the most drought-tolerant families. Grown primarily in non-flooded culture... The leaves are good for lūʻau. Mana ʻUlu makes yellow poi and is a favorite on Molokaʻi for making kūlolo and for table taro. It is one of the fastest maturing cultivars, at 7-8 months, similar to Lehua ʻEleʻele. Mana Keʻokeʻo produces large corms and is one of the best for making kūlolo." (Evans 2008 p.35-36)

"Piko taros have a characteristic leaf form in which the sinus (the "V" of the leaf blade) extends down to the petiole; thus they are easy to identify... These taros are the most late-maturing, averaging 12-15 months, and can be held in the patch for extended periods. This is considered one of the hardiest taro families, was the most common for flooded culture, and was the main taro family grown in Hālawa valley on Molokaʻi. Piko cultivars grow best in cooler areas having an abundance of cool water. They have a leathery looking corm skin and are resistant to root rot but are very susceptible to leaf blight. They can also be grown in non-flooded culture." (Evans 2008 p. 36)

"Lauloa is a family [of taro] known for its drought tolerance and is preferred for making kūlolo. It is said that of all the Hawaiʻi taros, Lauloa most resembled the legendary Hāloa, because it is tall and overshadows its keiki. Grown exclusively in non-flooded culture, it is also used for medicine... Lauloa Keʻokeʻo has been considered by some to produce the highest quality leaf for lūʻau of all the Hawaiian taro varieties." (Evans 2008 p.36)

"ʻEleʻele is a [taro] family grown in non-flooded culture and known for its red poi; these were the royal 'black' taros in ancient Hawaiʻi and were especially popular in the Kona area. The darkest of all Hawaiian taros, this is one of the favorites of growers in Hoʻolehua today. The two main types are ʻEleʻele Naioea and ʻEleʻele Mākoko, although some believe that Lauloa ʻEleʻele ʻUla belongs in this family. ʻEleʻele Naioea makes an excellent purple poi comparable to that from the Lahua family, but ʻEleʻele Mākoko has a higher yield." (Evans 2008 p. 36)

"Although most growers prefer to plant freshly harvested material, some choose to give smaller huli a 'jump-start' to increase their chances of survival. This is done either during drought conditions or to avoid weed competition by planting larger huli. It's done by holding huli in buckets or tanks containing a few inches of clean, fresh water for a few weeks. Plant them when they have two or three healthy leaves and a good mass of roots." (Evans 2008 p. 39)

"One way to get strong huli is to harvest the entire plant before it begins to 'drop down' or shrink in the later stages of growth. Use the strong juvenile huli from ʻohā as planting material for your next crop. Big, healthy, young huli usually produce big, healthy taro plants. Corms harvested early may be mature but probably are not at their full potential size." (Evans 2008 p. 39)

"In Hawaiʻi, taro is planted year-round, but it grows best when planted during spring and summer (April- September). During fall and winter (October-March), solar radiation is approximately half of that received during the warmer part of the year, and the cooler temperatures slow taro growth. Slower growth aids corm maturation and is desirable at the end of the growing cycle, but is not desirable when the plant is just beginning its life. Taro planted during winter has the disadvantage of slow corm growth in the early stages, which increases the incidence of disease at maturity and makes it harder to compete with weeds. Therefore, planting taro during the fall and winter months requires relatively more weeding and monitoring for disease. It is important to keep in mind that planting times and thus harvesting times vary with location and elevation because of differing climates." (Evans 2008 p. 41)

"If you plant huli close togehter (both within and between rows), the crop will yield more corm but have fewer ʻohā. Conversely, the further apart you plant huli, the more ʻohā you can expect to produce. However, if plants are too close together it will be difficult to work in the planting. Also, close plants may make it easier for insect pests to move among them, and if sunlight and air circulation is too restricted in the planting, moisture levels on the plants will be high and some diseases can occur more readily. Spacing huli too far apart, however, will make it difficult for the leaves to form a complete weed-suppressing canopy." (Evans 2008 p. 42)

"You will know when the taro is ready to be harvested when:
• the plants are at least 6 months old (or older, depending on the variety)
• most older leaves turn yellow and die off over a short period of time
• there are fewer new leaves being produced
• the new leaves that do grow are smaller than preceding ones
• most of the roots are gone or have shrunk (this will require you to pull some taro up and check for corm maturity)
• the top of the corm is above the soil surface and the diameter of the top of the corm is decreasing (forming a "neck").
These are all signs of maturity, when the plant's final energy is going into increasing the size of the corm to its maximum." (Evans 2008 p. 52)

ʻŌlelo Noʻeau (31)

From Pukui, ʻŌlelo Noʻeau (1983)

  • #83ʻAi no i kalo moʻa (One can eat cooked taro). The work is done; one can sit at ease and enjoy himself.
  • #263E ʻao lūʻau a kualima (Offer young taro leaves to the gods five times). Advice to one has erred and wishes to rectify his mistake. Young taro leaves oftern were substituted for pigs when making an offering to the gods. To remove sickness of mind or body, one made five separate offerings of young taro leaves.
  • #316E kanu i ka huli ʻoi hāʻule ka ua (Plant the taro stalks while there is rain). Do your work when opportunity affords.
  • #329E lawe i ke ō, he hinana ka iʻa kuhi lima (Take vegetable food; the hinana is a fish that can be caught in the hand). A suggestion to take taro, poi, potato, or breadfruit along on the journey and not worry about meats, which can be found along the way. First uttered by Pele in a chant about the winds of Kauaʻi.
  • #340E! Loaʻa aku la ke kalo, o ka ʻapowale (Say! You'll obtain a taro, the ʻapowale). You are wasting your time. A play on ʻapo-wale (grasp-at-nothing), a variety of taro.
  • #416Hakē ka paʻi ʻai o ka Malulani (The Malulani is overloaded with bundles of hard poi). An impolite reference to a pregnant woman. The Malulani was an inter-island ship.
  • #429Hālau ka hale; ʻohā ka ʻai (A big house; small taro to eat). A large house brings so many visitors that to feed them all, even immature taro must be used.
  • #666He kalo paʻa (Unpounded taro). A spinster or a bachelor.
  • #671He manu Mahoemua, he kalo pūʻali (When one plants in [the month of] Mahoemua, he will have irregularly shaped taro).
  • #814He mea ʻao lūʻau ʻia ke kānāwai (A law [of an ʻaumakua] can be removed with an offering of cooked taro leaves). An ʻaumakua could be propitiated by offering taro leaves and prayers for forgiveness.
  • #850He ʻohā pili wale (A young taro that attaches itself to an older corm). Said of a person who attaches himself to another in order to receive care. He is like a young taro that grows beside the parent plant but does not belong to it.
  • #892He pili kauawe paha ke kumu i moʻa ʻole ai ke kalo (Perhaps the reason for the partly cooked condition of the taro is because it is the one closest to the leaves that cover over the imu). Said of an imperfect or defective task, or of a person whose ideas are "half-baked."
  • #997Hilo ʻai lūʻau (Hilo, eater of taro greens). The people of Hilo were said to be fond of cooked taro greens. When storms came to Hilo it was impossible to obtain fish from the streams or the sea. The people had to be content with taro greens.
  • #1127Hui aku, hui mai, hui kalo me ka nāwao (Mixed there, mixed here, all mixed together are the cultivated and the wild taros). Said of a great mix-up.
  • #1232I maikaʻi ke kalo i ka ʻohā (The goodness of the taro is judged by the young plant it produces). Parents are often judged by the behavior of their children.
  • #1346Ka iʻa i nui ai o Kamehameha (The fish on which Kamehameha was raised). Taro greens. The Kamehameha mentioned here is the son of Kekaulike, ruler of Maui, not Kamehameha I, the conqueror. Once, when it was necessary for his personal attendants to be gone for the day, the chief, who was then a small child, was left in the care of his attendants' two young sons. Taro greens had been prepared and cooked for the royal child, because they were tender and easy to swallow. Kekaulike arrived unexpectedly and was displeased to see only taro greens instead of fish being given to his son. When the boys, who did not recognize him, explained that this was a very precious child and that the taro greens were fed him because they had no bones that would lodge in his throat, Kekaulike was pleased. Thus the little chief, who was reared at Pakaikai, Molokaʻi, became known as Kamehameha-nui-ʻai-lūʻau (Great Kemehameha, Eater-of-taro-greens).
  • #1360Ka iʻa lau nui o ka ʻāina (Big-leaved fish of the land). Lūʻau, or taro greens.
  • #1361Ka iʻa lauoho loloa o ka ʻāina (The long-haired fish of the land). Any vegetable eaten with poi, such as taro greens, hoʻiʻo or kikawaiō ferns, or sweet potato greens. Poetically, leaves are the oho or lauoho, hair, of plants.
  • #1447Kalo kanu o ka ʻāina (Taro planted on the land). Natives of the land from generations back.
  • #1509Kanu ke kalo i Welo, ʻaʻole e ulu nui ʻia e ka ʻohā (Plant taro in Welo and the offshoots will not be many). The corm of taro planted in the month of Welo grows very large but the offshoots are few.
  • #1700Ke hōʻole mai nei o Hāloa (Hāloa denies that). Hāloa is the god of taro. It was said that whatever business was discussed before an open poi bowl was denied by Hāloa. If a medical kahuna was called while eating, he took it as a sign that he was not the right person to treat the sick one. However, if he was told while eating that someone was dying, he was able to treat the illness, for Hāloa would deny the death.
  • #1735Ke kalo paʻa o Waiahole (The hard taro of Waiahole). A reminder not to treat others badly. One day, a man went to Waiahole, Oʻahu, to visit his sister, whom he had not seen for many years. She was absent, and her husband neither asked the stranger in nor offered him any food. When hunger possessed the visitor he asked if he might have some taro to eat. His brother-in-law directed him to his taro patches and told him to get some from there. The man went to the patches and then continued on his way. When the woman returned she was told of the visitor, and by her husband's description she knew that it was her brother. She rebuked him for his lack of hospitality. When they went to their taro patches they found all the taro pulled up and hacked to pieces.
  • #1953Lau-kapili. Lau-kapalala (Tremble-leaf. Broad-leaf). These were the names applied to the leaves of the very first taro plant, which according to legend was named Hāloa. Taro leaves are sometimes referred to poetically by these terms.
  • #1987Lele no ka ʻohe i kona lua (The ʻohe taro leaps into its own hole). Each person to his own place. From the legend of Kamiki, in which the hero called to the various taros by name, and each leaped into its own hole and stood there.
  • #2106Make no ke kalo a ola i ka naio (The taro dies but lives again in the pinworm). The matter may be thought dead, but it is likely to come alive again. Naio (pinworms) were sometimes found in poi and caused itching in the anal passage.
  • #2107Make no ke kalo a ola i ka palili (The taro may die but lives on in the young plants that it produces). One lives on in his children.
  • #2135Mānā, i ka puʻe kalo hoʻoneʻeneʻe a ka wai (Mānā, where the mounded taro moves in the water). Refers to Mānā, Kauaʻi. In ancient days there were five patches at Kolo, Mānā, in which deep water mound-planting was done for taro. As the plants grew, the rootless were allowed to spread undisturbed because they helped to hold the soil together. When the rainy season came, the whole area was flooded as far as Kalamaihiki, and it took weeks for the water to subside. The farmers built rafts of sticks and rushes, then dived into the water. They worked the bases of the taro mounds free and lifted them carefully, so as not to disturb the soil, to the rafts where they were secured. The weight of the mounds submerged the rafts but permitted the taro stalks to grow above water just as they did before the flood came. The rafts were tied together to form a large, floating field of taro.
  • #2395O ka ʻai no ka ʻai, o ka ʻiʻo kanaka ka iʻa (Food is here to be eaten, with only human flesh for meat). Said when there is nothing to eat with poi. There were once two boys of Kaʻū who won a riddling contest against a Kona man, the champion of the island of Hawaiʻi. In one riddle the boys claimed to be eating human flesh. The audience pondered this, since no meat was visible, and began to dispute the claim. Suddenly the boys popped wads of poi into their mouths and proceeded to lick their fingers---the "human flesh."
  • #2523ʻOno kāhi ʻao lūʻau me ke aloha pū (A little taro green is delicious when love is present). Even the plainest of fare is delicious when there is love.
  • #2652Pili ʻohā, he kāmau mai mawaho (A taro-offshoot relationship added to the outside of the corm). One who was not a relative, yet is a member of the household.
  • #2705Pūʻali kalo i ka wai ʻole (Taro, for lack of water, grows misshapen). For lack of care, one may become ill.
